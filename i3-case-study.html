It’s just that sudden feeling of being transported: Music as a digital possession after a romantic relationship ends

Overview

A four-author literature review examining how people process music as a digital possession after a breakup, conducted through the i3 Inclusion Institute at UT Austin and presented as a poster at iConference 2023. My role: co-author and project manager, coordinating the team’s research process alongside conducting the literature synthesis itself.

The Question

Most HCI research on breakups focuses on managing photos and social media connections after a split. Music gets left out of that conversation, even though a song can just as easily become a landmine. An algorithm surfacing the wrong track at the wrong moment, an old favorite suddenly unlistenable. The team’s guiding question: how do people process emotions through digital possessions like music after a relationship ends?

Methods

We ran a semi-structured literature review starting from two seed articles on music, memory, and emotion, then traced citations both backward and forward from there. That search returned 324 articles total. Three of us split the total and coded each one for relevance by reading abstracts; when relevance wasn’t obvious, the fourth author made the call. That process narrowed 324 down to 74 relevant articles, which we then redistributed, read in full, and grouped thematically — writing memos as we went to track how the groupings were forming.

Findings

Three themes came out of the analysis:

Locus of emotion in music — the distinction between what a song is designed to make you feel (external locus) and what you actually feel listening to it (internal locus), and how those two can align or clash based on personal history with a piece of music.

Hedonic shifting — this was the section I led. It looks at how music can pull someone’s emotional state in a different direction than intended: a song meant to comfort can instead drag someone back into a memory they weren’t ready for, and vice versa — sad music can sometimes lift someone’s mood rather than deepen a low one. This became one of the more design-relevant findings, since it pointed toward features that could let a streaming platform recognize when a track is doing more harm than good and give the user a way to intervene.

Personal connection to music — most existing research in this space uses non-popular or classical music specifically to control for personal associations, which is exactly backwards from how people actually listen day to day. The team’s takeaway was that researchers should stop trying to strip out personal connection to music and instead study it directly.

Implications

Overall, across all three themes, platforms like Spotify aren’t currently designed with breakups in mind at all. The team’s contribution was pointing toward concrete directions: letting users temporarily exclude an artist from recommendations, for example, that streaming services could use to reduce unwanted emotional collisions during a life transition.

Reflection

This project ran on a tight literature-review methodology that required real coordination. Splitting a 324-article corpus across three coders, resolving disagreements through a fourth reviewer, then reconvening to group everything thematically. That coordination work was as much a part of the project as the writing itself, which is part of why my role is credited as both project manager and researcher.
